Australia has one of the most effective cervical screening programs in the world. Since the program began in 1991, the country's cervical cancer rate has halved. In December 2017, the test changed  -  and it's now even more effective. This article walks through what the current test is, who it's for, and how to start.

### What changed in 2017: Pap test out, HPV test in

Until 2017, Australia used the Pap test  -  a check for abnormal cervical cells, repeated every two years.

In December 2017, the program switched to the **HPV test**, repeated every five years. Cancer Council Australia summarises why: *"The HPV test represents a significant improvement: it is more accurate, requiring testing only every 5 years, and is expected to reduce cervical cancer deaths by at least 20% more."*

The change makes sense once you know what causes cervical cancer. Almost all cases come from a long-term infection with high-risk HPV. The Pap test looked for the cell changes the virus eventually causes; the HPV test looks for the virus itself, catching trouble years earlier.

| **Pap test (pre-2017)** | **HPV test (2017 onwards)** |
|---|---|
| Looked for abnormal cells | Looks for the virus that causes them |
| Every 2 years | Every 5 years |
| Ages 18 to 69 | Ages 25 to 74 |

### Are you eligible?

You're eligible for cervical screening in Australia if **all** of the following are true:

- You are aged **25 to 74**
- You have a **cervix**
- You have **ever been sexually active**

Cancer Council Australia is explicit: *"The program applies regardless of sexual orientation, gender identity, vaccination status, sexual activity level, or menopausal status."*

In practice, that means:

- **Trans men and non-binary people with a cervix:** yes, this includes you
- **Vaccinated against HPV:** yes  -  the vaccine doesn't cover every high-risk type
- **In a same-sex relationship:** yes
- **Past menopause:** yes, until age 74

### Self-collection: a real option since 2022

Since July 2022, Australia has offered **self-collection** as a fully equivalent alternative to clinician collection.

You collect your own sample using a simple swab, under healthcare provider guidance. Cancer Council Australia describes it: *"You collect your own sample using a simple swab under healthcare provider guidance  -  equally effective and suitable for those uncomfortable with clinician collection."*

For many people, self-collection removes the biggest barrier to screening: discomfort with the speculum exam, modesty concerns, or past medical trauma.

**Important:** self-collection isn't appropriate if you have **symptoms** (unusual bleeding, unusual discharge, or pain). Symptoms need a clinician examination, not a swab.

### How often?

If your HPV test is normal (HPV not detected), the next screening is in **five years**.

The National Cancer Screening Register sends invitations and reminders by mail. If you've missed an invitation, you can call the NCSR on **1800 627 701** to check your status and get back on track.

### What happens at the appointment

Most appointments take about ten minutes. You can request a female provider if that's important to you. The full process  -  what to wear, what happens in the room, what comes afterward  -  is covered in its own article.

[Read: What to expect at your screening appointment](/learn/screening-appointment)

### What about your results?

You'll get your results, usually by SMS or letter, within a few weeks.

**HPV detected does not mean cancer.** It means the test caught something to follow up on, while there is plenty of time to act.

[Read: Understanding your screening results](/learn/understanding-results)

### How to book

Three ways to start:

1. **Talk to your GP.** Most appointments are bulk-billed. If you don't have a regular GP, any GP can do this.
2. **No GP yet?** Find a screening provider near you using our clinic finder.
3. **Missed an invitation?** Call the National Cancer Screening Register on **1800 627 701**.
